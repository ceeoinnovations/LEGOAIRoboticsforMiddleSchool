Overview

Browser: Chrome or Edge only, on a computer or Android phone — not Safari, iPhone/iPad, or Firefox. The app needs Web Bluetooth to talk to the Double Motors which (as of right now) those don't support.

Nothing is saved automatically: there's no server and no accounts — every recorded motion and trained model lives only in the browser tab's memory, and a reload clears everything. Both classes now have a manual save: Class 1 has a "Save Examples" tab with "Save My Data"/"Load Saved Data" (raw recordings only — students click Train/Retrain again after loading), and Class 2, Lesson 1 has "Export"/"Import" for pooling mystery motions across the class. These are two different file formats, so importing one where the other belongs gets rejected — the app now tells students which tab to use when that happens. Neither save happens automatically, so still plan around it: either keep the tab open across a multi-day unit, or have students save before closing the tab.

Two knobs worth knowing before you're in front of a class: the Recognition threshold slider (in the sidebar, default 50%) controls how similar to the examples a cast needs to be to be recognized, and applies everywhere a cast gets tested — Lessons 1, 2, 3, and Data Cleaning — not just Lesson 1. The Number of groups slider (Class 2 Lesson 1, default 3) tells k-means how many clusters to look for. Both are meant to be tuned by the class, not left on defaults.

How the AI actually works, in one paragraph: every Class 1 lesson (0-5) runs on one algorithm, K-Nearest Neighbors. “Training” just means saving example recordings; recognizing a new cast means measuring how close it is to those saved examples and picking the closest match. Nothing is hidden — any lesson's result traces back to “which saved examples ended up closest, and was that close enough (the threshold)?” Class 2 Lesson 1 uses a different algorithm, k-means clustering, which groups unlabeled recordings by mutual closeness without ever being told what the groups are. Class 2 Lesson 2 then reuses the same KNN machinery from Class 1, just with the k-means groups as its labels instead of human-given ones. Both are implemented from scratch in wand_ml.py — no scikit-learn, no cloud API, no pre-trained models — specifically so nothing here is a black box.

Class 1

Lesson 0: Build Your Wand

Student challenge: “Build a magic wand and create different types of spells!”

What's actually happening

No AI yet — this lesson is entirely about the hardware. The Double Motor's built-in IMU (accelerometer + gyroscope) streams six numbers continuously ([ax, ay, az, gx, gy, gz]) once connected; every later lesson's “recording” is just buffering that stream while a button is held.

What “working” looks like

The light blinks and beeps (not just glows) before it shows up in the browser's device picker, the wand is built securely enough that the sensor doesn't shift mid-cast, and each student has picked one consistent grip/orientation they'll reuse for the rest of the unit.

Common snags & fixes

  • Web Bluetooth only works in Chrome or Edge, on a computer or Android phone — confirm your classroom's devices support it before the period starts, not during.
  • Pairing can be done with or without the color cards.
  • Without a color card, press the double motor's light/button once. It should start blinking white. Press the "Connect" button on the website and a popup should appear offering to connect to something over bluetooth - pick the Double Motor.
  • With a color card, press the button once and it will blink and beep white. Tap the color card and now the light/button will be lit solidly with that color. Then press a second time, and it will beep again and blink in color. Now if you open the connect dialog box, it will list the color of the device---useful if you have a classroom full of beeping double motors so that everyone connects to their own motor and not someone else's
  • NOTHING prevents the students from connecting to someone else's double motor either accidentally or intentionally by keeping the Bluetooth window open and pairing faster than their classmate can navigate the interface. If something is weird, check if the students are actually connected to the motor they are holding.
  • Flaky pairing usually means Bluetooth interference — move the wand closer to the laptop and away from other Bluetooth devices in the room.
  • A loose or inconsistent grip is the single biggest cause of “spell not recognized” two lessons from now — worth getting right here rather than fixing later.

Discussion angle

The two “Think Like” prompts (which motions are easiest/hardest to tell apart, how many examples give good accuracy) are predictions students can't check yet. Consider having them write a guess down now, then revisit it after Lesson 1's results.

Class 1

Lesson 1: Teach Your Wand a Spell

Student challenge: “Teach your wand to recognize one spell, just by showing it examples!”

What's actually happening

This is K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) in its simplest form. Each held-button recording becomes one saved data point — a 6-axis motion trace, resampled to a fixed length (resample_trace in wand_ml.py) so a fast cast and a slow cast can still be compared fairly. “Training” doesn't compute anything clever; it just stores the examples. Recognizing a test cast means measuring its distance to those saved examples and converting that into a confidence score — the Recognition threshold slider is simply “how close counts as a match.”

What “working” looks like

6-8 examples that overlap closely on the “Compare your examples” chart, and a test cast recognized comfortably above the threshold (default 50%).

Common snags & fixes

  • If a cast isn't recognized, lower the threshold slider before assuming the training data itself is bad — it's often just a strictness setting.
  • The overlay chart is the fastest diagnostic in the whole app: if the lines don't sit on top of each other, that's almost always why recognition is failing.
  • Flat time at the start of a line (before the spike) means the button was held before the motion started — that's Lesson 5's “dead time” problem showing up early. Fine to mention now and revisit properly later.

Discussion angle

“How many examples does a student need before they really learn something?” is a genuine sample-size question with no fixed right answer — but students should notice diminishing returns (going from 2 to 6 examples helps a lot; 6 to 12 helps much less) if you have time to let them experiment.

Class 1

Lesson 2: Can Your Friend Use It?

Student challenge: “Can your friend use your gesture to do your spell?”

What's actually happening

Nothing is retrained here — this lesson exists purely to expose a generalization gap. The model still only knows the original caster's examples from Lesson 1; a different person's motion is run through it unchanged.

What “working” looks like

Counterintuitively, a low recognition rate is the correct, expected outcome on this page. If a friend's cast is recognized easily, that's fine too, but there's less to talk about in Lesson 3.

Common snags & fixes

  • Students will instinctively want to fix it immediately — hold them off. There's no retrain button on this page on purpose; the failure is the data point Lesson 3 needs.
  • One low score could be a fluke; a second or third tester (if time allows) makes the pattern more convincing before moving on.

Discussion angle

This is the natural spot to zoom out from the wand and talk about real-world training bias — voice assistants that struggle with some accents, or vision models trained mostly on one demographic. The wand makes it a shared, felt experience rather than an abstract example.

Class 1

Lesson 3: Improve It — Data Bias

Student challenge: “Fix the bias — retrain your wand so it works for your friend too!”

What's actually happening

Fine-tuning. The friend's Lesson 2 attempts get added into the same training set (originals are kept, not replaced), and the KNN classifier “retrains” — really just re-saves the combined set. The Before/After bars are two accuracy snapshots of the same test, run against the model before and after that addition.

What “working” looks like

The After bar clearly higher than Before. A small or absent improvement isn't necessarily a bug — see snags below.

Common snags & fixes

  • One added example is often not enough to close a generalization gap; if After barely moves, add more of the friend's attempts (back to Lesson 2 to record a few more) rather than repeatedly retraining on the same single example.
  • If accuracy still doesn't improve much even with more examples, the problem may be layered with Lesson 5's dead-time issue rather than diversity alone — worth checking both.

Discussion angle

“Is one friend's data enough, or should you ask a whole group?” scales nicely into a real classroom activity — pool a few students' correction data and compare how much bigger the fix is with 3 people's examples versus 1.

Class 1

Lesson 4: Who Cast It?

Student challenge: “Can the AI tell you and your friend apart, just by how you both cast the same spell?”

What's actually happening

A second, independent KNN classifier, trained on the exact same recordings as Lessons 1-2 (no new capture step) but with a different label attached — instead of “is this a Fireball?” it learns “whose Fireball is this?” Same algorithm as every Class 1 lesson, just pointed at a different question.

What “working” looks like

Correct guesses that improve as more casters and casts get added to the pool — this lesson is more data-hungry than earlier ones, since it needs to tell multiple people apart rather than recognize one person's motion.

Common snags & fixes

  • If training reports it needs more data, that's expected, not an error — send students back to Lesson 2 to record a few more attempts (their own or a friend's).
  • The “Correct?” column only matches if the caster's name is typed exactly as it was in Lesson 2's “Tester's name” field — spelling/capitalization mismatches will look like wrong guesses that aren't.
  • Two people getting confused with each other often means their casting styles are genuinely similar — try casting more differently on purpose, then retrain, and see if that widens the gap.

Discussion angle

“If AI can recognize how you move, what else could it learn about you without asking?” is a real hook into behavioral biometrics — gait recognition, keystroke dynamics, how phones fingerprint users by how they hold or swipe them. Worth pausing the lesson for an actual conversation here rather than treating it as just another on-page prompt.

Class 1

Lesson 5: Improve It — Data Cleaning

Student challenge: “Clean up your training data — trim the dead time before the real spell begins!”

What's actually happening

A different data-quality fix from Lesson 3 — this one is about how clean each individual recording is, not who's represented. If a student held the button for a moment before actually moving, that flat “dead time” gets recorded as part of the example and confuses the distance comparison KNN relies on. The app suggests an automatic crop point per recording; students can drag it before reapplying and retraining.

What “working” looks like

The After bar higher than Before once crops are applied and the model is retrained — ideally without having added any new examples, which is the point (same data, cleaner).

Common snags & fixes

  • The suggested crop point is a guess, not a rule — if it visibly disagrees with what the chart shows, trust the chart.
  • Over-cropping can cut into the real spell motion, not just the dead time, and will make accuracy worse, not better. If that happens, drag the marker back and retrain again.
  • Students can revisit “Load My Examples” any time before committing, so encourage checking crops rather than applying on the first guess.

Discussion angle

Make the Lesson 3 vs. Lesson 5 contrast explicit — Lesson 3 fixed who was represented in the training data; this lesson fixes how clean each individual recording is. Two different categories of “bad data,” both worth naming.

Class 2

Class 2, Lesson 1: Discover Hidden Magic Styles

Student challenge: “Let the AI find its own groups — no labels, no hints!”

What's actually happening

Unsupervised learning via k-means clustering (KMeansCluster in wand_ml.py, also from scratch). Students record unlabeled “mystery motions” and the algorithm groups them by mutual similarity — it's never told what the groups are, only how many to look for (the “Number of groups” slider). The scatter plot is a simplified 2D view (roughly: how big vs. how twisty the motion was); the algorithm itself compares the full 6-axis motion shape, so two dots that look close on the plot can represent motions that are more different than they appear.

Classroom logistics

This lesson works best when a teacher secretly assigns 2-3 distinct motions across the class, so there's real hidden structure for k-means to find rather than genuinely random motions. Because there's no server or accounts, pooling data across a whole class means each student exports their motions to a JSON file and hands it to whoever will run “Find Patterns” — over Google Drive, your classroom app, or email, whatever your class already uses for file handoff — who then imports every file before Step 3.

What “working” looks like

Distinct, well-separated clusters on the scatter plot when k matches however many motions were actually (secretly) assigned. The result also reports a “spread score” — k-means inertia, i.e. the sum of each motion's squared distance to its cluster's center — where lower means tighter, more consistent groups. It's only meaningful relative to other runs on the same set of motions, so use it to compare different k values against each other, or to check whether the optional crop tool actually tightened things up; it's not a number with meaning on its own.

Common snags & fixes

  • Motions that are too similar to each other are hard for the algorithm (and for students eyeballing the plot) to separate — this is expected, not a failure.
  • A k that doesn't match the real number of groups will split things oddly; encourage trying a few different values rather than treating the first result as final.
  • The optional “Clean Up Your Mystery Motions” crop tool exists for the same dead-time issue as Lesson 5's supervised counterpart — it's collapsed by default and only worth opening if the groups look messier than expected.

Discussion angle

Have students privately write down how they'd group the motions before clicking “Find Patterns” — a lightweight pre-registration exercise that makes comparing their guess to the algorithm's grouping much more interesting.

Class 2

Class 2, Lesson 2: Make New Spells

Student challenge: “Turn the AI's discoveries into brand-new spells you can cast!”

What's actually happening

This closes the loop between the two classes. It takes the clusters k-means found in Lesson 5 (Class 2), lets students name each one, and then reuses the exact same KNN recognition machinery from Class 1 — just with the discovered clusters as labels instead of labels a human assigned up front.

What “working” looks like

The wand's light/beep feedback correctly distinguishes each named spell during casting.

Common snags & fixes

  • If a spell isn't being recognized, the root cause is usually back in Lesson 5 — that cluster was probably messy or overlapped with another group on the scatter plot, not a problem with the naming step itself.
  • Encourage casting both carefully and sloppily once spells are saved, to see how much slack the recognizer actually has.

Discussion angle

“What would you call a motion the AI grouped together, before you even tried it?” is a nice bridge to how humans routinely impose labels onto statistical structure after the fact — which is literally what just happened in this lesson.

Standards Alignment

This progression follows the same conceptual arc as the 2026 CSTA AI guidance: students first learn how AI systems work, then experiment with how data changes model behavior, and finally reason about fairness, bias, and appropriate use — using evidence gathered from their own trained models rather than an abstract lecture.

If you're teaching a general middle school CS class

The progression is a strong match for the foundational AI standards because students are:

  • observing how machine learning behaves rather than treating it as a black box,
  • testing hypotheses about why models make particular decisions,
  • improving model performance by modifying the training data,
  • discussing fairness, representation, and bias through evidence rather than lecture.

If you're teaching an AI elective or advanced high school course

The same activities also introduce several AI Specialty standards:

  • S1-AIN-DD-02 – Modify AI system training data to improve fairness and accuracy. Lesson 3's fine-tune-with-a-friend's-examples activity and Lesson 5's crop-and-retrain activity both directly demonstrate this.
  • S1-AIN-HR-09 – Analyze the potential biases and limitations of AI systems, supported by Lesson 2's generalization-gap discussion and Lesson 4's privacy/behavioral-biometrics discussion.
  • S2-AIN-DD-01 – Analyze problems that can be addressed using unsupervised learning, addressed directly by Class 2 Lesson 1's clustering activity.

S1-AIN-DD-03 (“create an application using a prebuilt supervised learning model”) does not apply here, since this app's classifier is intentionally built entirely from scratch on raw data. Check back at engineeringwithbricks.com for other activities that meet this standard using challenges with pre-trained models!

AP Computer Science Principles

This sequence also aligns well with AP CSP, particularly:

  • Big Idea 2: Data — students collect, compare, and improve datasets while seeing how data quality affects computational outcomes.
  • Big Idea 5: Impact of Computing — students examine bias, fairness, reliability, and appropriate use of AI through firsthand experimentation rather than abstract discussion.
